\section{RAMP Brinkman Interpolation}
\label{sec:app_topology_ramp_heaviside}
For visualization, the Brinkman resistance in Eq.~\ref{eq:brinkman_interpolation} can be normalized as
\begin{equation}
	\hat{\alpha}(\bar{\gamma}) =
	\frac{\alpha(\bar{\gamma})-\alpha_{fluid}}
	{\alpha_{solid}-\alpha_{fluid}} .
	\label{eq:normalized_brinkman_resistance}
\end{equation}
When $\alpha_{fluid}=0$, this becomes $\hat{\alpha}=\alpha(\bar{\gamma})/\alpha_{solid}$. With the $q$ convention used in this thesis, small $q$ values make most intermediate densities comparatively fluid-like except very close to $\bar{\gamma}=0$, while larger $q$ values increase the resistance over a wider part of the intermediate-density range.

\end{figure}

\section{Active and Passive Density Regions}
\label{sec:app_topology_active_passive_regions}
Figure~\ref{fig:filter_domain_schematic} sketches the distinction between active design cells and passive non-design extensions. Active cells are updated by MMA. Passive-fluid cells, such as inlet and outlet extensions, are fixed to $\bar{\gamma}=1$ but remain part of the flow domain. Passive-solid cells, when present, are fixed to $\bar{\gamma}=0$.

\section{DG0 Density Filtering and Active-Mask Normalization}
\label{sec:app_topology_filtering}
The density filter in Eq.~\ref{eq:dg_helmholtz_filter} is applied to the DG0 design field before projection. Near fixed inlet and outlet extensions, the active-mask normalization prevents passive-fluid cells from smoothing into the active design domain.

To prevent fixed inlet and outlet extensions from smoothing into the active design domain, the implementation filters only the active contribution and normalizes by the filtered active mask:
\begin{equation}
	\tilde{\gamma}
	=
	\frac{\mathcal{H}(M\gamma)}
	{\mathcal{H}(M)} .
	\label{eq:active_mask_filter}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathcal{H}$ denotes the filter solve and $M$ is the active-cell indicator. After projection, passive-cell density bounds are restored. During sensitivity assembly, the same chain is traversed in reverse so that MMA receives gradients only for active design degrees of freedom.

\section{Inlet Turbulence Conversion}
\label{sec:app_topology_inlet_turbulence}
When an inlet turbulence intensity $I$ and turbulence length scale $\ell$ are provided, the code first estimates an eddy-viscosity ratio using the standard $k$--$\varepsilon$ inlet relations,
\begin{equation}
	k=\frac{3}{2}(UI)^2,
	\qquad
	\epsilon=C_\mu^{3/4}\frac{k^{3/2}}{\ell},
	\qquad
	\frac{\nu_t}{\nu}
	=
	C_\mu^{1/4}\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}\,
	I\,\frac{U\ell}{\nu}.
	\label{eq:inlet_turbulence_to_viscosity_ratio}
\end{equation}
If the Reynolds number and length-scale ratio are supplied instead, $U\ell/\nu$ is replaced by $Re\,(\ell/L)$.

The resulting ratio is converted to the SA inlet working variable by solving the SA viscosity relation
\begin{equation}
	\nu_t = \tilde{\nu} f_{v1},
	\qquad
	f_{v1}=\frac{\chi^3}{\chi^3+c_{v1}^3},
	\qquad
	\chi=\frac{\tilde{\nu}}{\nu}.
	\label{eq:sa_viscosity_ratio_conversion}
\end{equation}
This conversion is only an inlet initialization and boundary-condition device. The SA transport equation still determines the interior $\tilde{\nu}$ field during the forward solve.

\section{Forward-Solver Controls}
\label{sec:app_topology_forward_controls}
The optimization code uses IPCS and Picard updates for robust forward progress, then assembles the final frozen-viscosity residual used by the adjoint. Some benchmark configurations also use SNES recovery schedules, convection continuation, and accepted-residual criteria during difficult intermediate designs. These controls are numerical devices for obtaining a usable final state; the frozen adjoint in Chapter~\ref{ch:topology_optimization_methodology} is assembled from the final steady residual in Eq.~\ref{eq:penalized_momentum_weak}, not from a differentiation of every IPCS or Picard substep.
