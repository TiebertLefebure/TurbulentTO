\chapter{Turbulence Modeling}
\label{ch:turbulence_modeling}

\section{Introduction to Fluid Dynamics and Turbulence}
Fluid dynamics is governed by the Navier-Stokes equations, which describe the conservation of mass and momentum for a viscous continuum fluid \cite{White2006}. In this introductory text, these equations are presented using index notation to clearly illustrate the individual tensor components. The indices $i$ and $j$ represent the three spatial dimensions, and a repeated index within a term implies summation over all spatial directions. In subsequent chapters dealing with finite element implementation, this notation shifts to standard vector calculus to align with the FEniCS Unified Form Language syntax.

The fundamental principle of mass conservation is expressed by the continuity equation. For a general compressible fluid, this is defined as:
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial (\rho u_i)}{\partial x_i} = 0
\end{equation}
where $\rho$ is the fluid density. For most liquid cooling applications and low-speed fluid manifolds, the fluid can be assumed incompressible. Under this assumption, $\rho$ is constant in space and time, and the continuity equation reduces to
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_i} = 0 .
	\label{eq:continuity}
\end{equation}

For an incompressible Newtonian fluid with constant dynamic viscosity $\mu$, the conservation of momentum is expressed as:
\begin{equation}
	\rho \left( \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial t} + u_j \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} \right) = -\frac{\partial p}{\partial x_i} + \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} \left( \mu \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} \right)
	\label{eq:navier_stokes}
\end{equation}
where $p$ represents the static pressure. In principle, solving Equation~\ref{eq:navier_stokes} directly yields the exact flow field. This approach is known as Direct Numerical Simulation (DNS). However, as the Reynolds number ($Re$) increases, the flow transitions into a chaotic turbulent state characterized by a broad spectrum of spatial and temporal scales \cite{Pope2000}. Resolving the smallest turbulent eddies requires extremely fine spatial grids. For industrial applications, including electronics cooling manifolds, DNS is computationally prohibitive. Engineers therefore rely on turbulence modeling to predict the averaged effects of these fluctuations without resolving the full turbulent spectrum \cite{Versteeg2007, Altair}.

\section{Reynolds-Averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) Equations}
The most common approach to turbulence modeling in engineering is the Reynolds-Averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) framework, based on the theories originally introduced by Osborne Reynolds \cite{Pope2000}. The core principle of RANS is the Reynolds decomposition, which separates instantaneous flow variables into a time-averaged mean component ($\bar{u}_i$, $\bar{p}$) and a fluctuating component ($u'_i$, $p'$):
\begin{equation}
	u_i = \bar{u}_i + u'_i \quad \text{and} \quad p = \bar{p} + p' .
\end{equation}

Figure~\ref{fig:reynolds_decomposition} illustrates this separation for velocity and pressure signals measured at a fixed point in a turbulent flow. The instantaneous signal contains rapid turbulent fluctuations, while the dashed mean line represents the slowly varying quantity retained by the RANS equations. By construction, the mean of the fluctuating component is zero, but products of fluctuations, such as $\overline{u'_i u'_j}$, remain non-zero and generate the Reynolds-stress terms.

\begin{figure}[htpb]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{Reynolds-decomposition}
	\caption{Reynolds decomposition of velocity and pressure signals into mean and fluctuating components. Adapted from the Fluid Mechanics on the Web Chapter 6 material based on White's viscous-flow treatment \cite{OnlineFlowCalculatorWhiteCh6}.}
	\label{fig:reynolds_decomposition}
\end{figure}

Substituting these decomposed variables into the standard Navier-Stokes equations and taking the time average yields the RANS momentum equations:
\begin{equation}
	\rho \left( \frac{\partial \bar{u}_i}{\partial t} + \bar{u}_j \frac{\partial \bar{u}_i}{\partial x_j} \right) = -\frac{\partial \bar{p}}{\partial x_i} + \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} \left( \mu \frac{\partial \bar{u}_i}{\partial x_j} - \rho \overline{u'_i u'_j} \right) .
	\label{eq:rans_momentum}
\end{equation}
The averaging process introduces a new term, $-\rho \overline{u'_i u'_j}$, known as the Reynolds stress tensor. This tensor represents the momentum transfer caused by turbulent fluctuations. The introduction of this term creates a mathematical closure problem because the RANS equations now contain more unknowns than available physical equations \cite{Versteeg2007}.

\subsection{The Boussinesq Hypothesis and the Isotropic Assumption}
To close the system and solve for the mean flow, most RANS models rely on the Boussinesq eddy viscosity hypothesis \cite{Pope2000}. This hypothesis assumes that turbulent momentum transfer is analogous to molecular momentum transfer. It relates the Reynolds stresses linearly to the mean velocity gradients using a scalar turbulent eddy viscosity ($\mu_t$):
\begin{equation}
	-\rho \overline{u'_i u'_j} = \mu_t \left( \frac{\partial \bar{u}_i}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial \bar{u}_j}{\partial x_i} \right) - \frac{2}{3} \rho k \delta_{ij}
	\label{eq:boussinesq}
\end{equation}
where $k$ is the turbulent kinetic energy and $\delta_{ij}$ is the Kronecker delta. The primary task of any Boussinesq-based turbulence model is to calculate this local eddy viscosity.

It is crucial to highlight a fundamental physical limitation of Equation~\ref{eq:boussinesq}: the Boussinesq hypothesis assumes that $\mu_t$ is an isotropic scalar quantity. It maps all turbulence effects into one scalar multiplier aligned with the mean strain rate. While this assumption is often adequate for attached shear flows, it is less reliable when curvature, rotation, or separation create Reynolds-stress anisotropy. Curved ducts and bends are especially relevant here because centrifugal effects redistribute momentum across the cross-section and can create secondary motion that a scalar eddy-viscosity closure cannot represent exactly \cite{Rumsey2000,Rumsey2001}.

\section{Boundary Layer Theory and Near-Wall Treatment}
Before selecting a specific turbulence equation to calculate $\mu_t$, the physical structure of a turbulent boundary layer must be considered. The boundary layer is traditionally divided into three regions based on the non-dimensional wall distance ($y^+$) and non-dimensional velocity ($u^+$):
\begin{equation}
	y^+ = \frac{y u_\tau}{\nu}, \quad u^+ = \frac{\bar{u}}{u_\tau}
\end{equation}
where $y$ is the distance from the wall, $\nu$ is the kinematic viscosity, and $u_\tau = \sqrt{\tau_w/\rho}$ is the friction velocity derived from the wall shear stress ($\tau_w$) \cite{Pope2000}.

Figure~\ref{fig:near_wall_treatment} shows the usual wall-unit subdivision used when discussing RANS wall treatment. The viscous sublayer lies closest to the wall and is controlled mainly by molecular viscosity. The buffer layer is a transition region where molecular and turbulent shear are both important. Farther from the wall, the logarithmic layer follows the classical law-of-the-wall behavior, while the outer wake region becomes increasingly sensitive to pressure-gradient and outer-flow effects \cite{AltairNearWall2024}.

\begin{figure}[htpb]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{Near-wall-treatment}
	\caption{Near-wall turbulent boundary-layer regions in wall units. Adapted from Altair's AcuSolve near-wall modeling documentation \cite{AltairNearWall2024}.}
	\label{fig:near_wall_treatment}
\end{figure}

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{The viscous sublayer ($y^+ < 5$):} Immediately adjacent to the wall, turbulent fluctuations are damped by kinematic constraints. Viscous shear dominates, and the velocity profile is approximately linear ($u^+ = y^+$).
	\item \textbf{The buffer layer ($5 < y^+ < 30$):} A transition region where viscous and turbulent shear stresses are both important.
	\item \textbf{The log-law region ($y^+ > 30$):} Farther from the wall, turbulence dominates and the velocity follows a logarithmic distribution controlled by the von Karman constant \cite{Versteeg2007,Spalding1961}.
\end{enumerate}

High-Reynolds models, such as the standard $k-\epsilon$ model, use empirical wall functions to bridge the gap between the wall and the log-law region, requiring the first computational mesh element to be placed at $y^+ > 30$. For topology optimization, where solid-fluid boundaries continuously evolve during the optimization loop, empirical wall functions introduce severe mathematical discontinuities and numerical instabilities. Therefore, a wall-resolved model that integrates the governing equations through the viscous sublayer ($y^+ \approx 1$) is required for robust gradient-based optimization.

\section{The Spalart-Allmaras One-Equation Model}
While two-equation models such as $k-\epsilon$ or $k-\omega$ solve separate transport equations for turbulent kinetic energy and a turbulent length scale, they introduce highly non-linear source terms. These terms frequently cause numerical instabilities during the adjoint sensitivity analysis required for topology optimization. To achieve a trade-off between physical accuracy and computational efficiency, this thesis utilizes the Spalart-Allmaras (SA) model, originally developed in 1992 for aerodynamic flows with adverse pressure gradients \cite{Spalart1992}. The SA model solves a single transport equation for a working variable, $\tilde{\nu}$, which is subsequently linked to the turbulent kinematic eddy viscosity ($\nu_t=\mu_t/\rho$) \cite{Spalart1994}.

The transport equation for the standard SA model is given by:
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\partial \tilde{\nu}}{\partial t} + \bar{u}_j \frac{\partial \tilde{\nu}}{\partial x_j} = c_{b1} \tilde{S} \tilde{\nu} - c_{w1} f_w \left( \frac{\tilde{\nu}}{d} \right)^2 + \frac{1}{\sigma} \left[ \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} \left( (\nu + \tilde{\nu}) \frac{\partial \tilde{\nu}}{\partial x_j} \right) + c_{b2} \left( \frac{\partial \tilde{\nu}}{\partial x_j} \right)^2 \right] .
	\label{eq:sa_transport}
\end{equation}
The terms on the right-hand side represent production, destruction, and diffusion of the turbulent working variable, respectively. The production term relies on a modified vorticity scalar, $\tilde{S}$, so turbulence generation in the SA model is fundamentally tied to shear. The destruction term is explicitly a function of $d$, the distance to the nearest wall. This makes the SA model a wall-resolved low-Reynolds model that integrates through the viscous sublayer without requiring empirical wall functions or a second length-scale equation \cite{NasaTurb}.

At this stage of the thesis, the SA model is treated as a turbulence closure rather than an implementation object. Therefore, Chapter~\ref{ch:turbulence_modeling} only states the main transport equation and its physical role. Detailed constants, damping functions, linearization choices, and weak forms are deferred to Chapter~\ref{ch:fenics_implementation}, where the FEniCS implementation is discussed.

\section{Evaluation of Curvature Effects and the Dean Number}
\label{sec:curvature_effects}
Despite its computational advantages for topology optimization, the standard SA model relies on a single length scale tied to the nearest wall distance. It lacks an explicit rotation/curvature correction and remains constrained by the Boussinesq eddy-viscosity assumption. It can therefore struggle to capture the anisotropic physics of streamline curvature.

When fluid passes through a bend, centrifugal forces induce secondary motion perpendicular to the main flow direction. To quantify the strength of these curvature effects, the dimensionless Dean number ($De$) is utilized:
\begin{equation}
	De = Re_d \sqrt{\frac{d}{2R_c}}
\end{equation}
where $d$ is the characteristic length of the cross-section, such as the pipe diameter $D$ or hydraulic diameter $D_h$, $R_c$ is the radius of curvature, and $Re_d$ is the bulk Reynolds number based on that same characteristic length \cite{Dean1927}. A higher Dean number indicates a stronger cross-sectional influence of centrifugal forces.

The Dean number is academically relevant here because it separates curvature severity from Reynolds number alone. However, it should not be interpreted as a universal threshold for SA validity. The breakdown of an eddy-viscosity model depends on geometry, separation, mesh resolution, boundary conditions, and the validation quantity being compared. In this thesis, the Dean number is used as a physically meaningful way to bracket the curvature range of the benchmark cases.

For mildly curved flows, the SA model can perform relatively well. Apalowo \cite{Apalowo2022} evaluated a $90^\circ$ circular pipe bend with a Dean number of $De \approx 9,000$ at $Re_D \approx 34,000$. Under these moderate conditions, where $R_c=7D$, the SA model provided an acceptable approximation of the streamwise velocity profiles compared to higher-order models and experiments. This moderate-curvature agreement is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:apalowo_profiles}.

\begin{figure}[htpb]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{Apalowo-SA-performance}
	\caption{Streamwise velocity profiles in a $90^\circ$ pipe bend ($R_c = 7D$, $Re_D \approx 34,000$, $De \approx 9,000$). The SA model performs relatively well for mild curvature. Reprinted from Apalowo \cite{Apalowo2022}.}
	\label{fig:apalowo_profiles}
\end{figure}

As curvature tightens and the Dean number increases, the limitations of the isotropic Boussinesq assumption become more severe. Abuhatira et al. \cite{Abuhatira2021} evaluated turbulent flow through a $90^\circ$ pipe bend with $R_c=2.0D$ and $Re_D=60,000$, corresponding to $De \approx 30,000$. In this configuration, the SA model mischaracterized the near-wall velocity profiles at the bend exit, particularly close to the inner wall, while two-equation models resolved the measured trend more accurately. This provides supporting evidence that strong curvature can expose the limitations of the standard SA closure, but it is not treated as proof of a universal failure threshold.

Adapted from Abuhatira et al. \cite{Abuhatira2021}.}

The literature therefore provides useful bracketing evidence: SA can be acceptable for mild curvature and can fail for stronger curvature. The thesis-specific evidence must come from the Ansys comparisons below, which evaluate the geometries and quantities most relevant to the present optimization study.

\section{Separated Flow Without Curvature}
\label{sec:separated_flow_without_curvature}
Curvature is not the only mechanism that can expose the limitations of a scalar eddy-viscosity model. The SA model can perform well for attached wall-bounded flows, where the near-wall production and destruction balance is the dominant modeling requirement. Fully developed channel flow is therefore not treated as a model-limitation case in this chapter, since it is an attached wall-bounded configuration for which SA is expected to behave well; it is instead used later as an implementation-verification benchmark.

Separated internal flows provide a more demanding non-curved test. A backward-facing step removes bend curvature from the problem but introduces a sudden expansion, free shear-layer development, recirculation, and downstream reattachment. The resulting topology is commonly described in terms of the separated shear layer, the reattachment zone, and the redeveloping turbulent boundary layer after reattachment \cite{EatonJohnston1981,KimKlineJohnston1980}. This is relevant for topology-optimized manifolds and diffuser-like designs, where local separation may occur even when the geometry is not dominated by pipe-bend curvature. Because there is no curvature radius, the Dean number is not meaningful for this case. The standard metric is instead the normalized reattachment length $x_r/h$, where $h$ is the step height.

Separated flow is difficult for RANS closures because the modeled quantity is the time-averaged flow, while all turbulent fluctuations and their transport appear only through closure assumptions. The reliability issue is therefore not that every RANS model immediately fails after separation, but that several assumptions become weaker at the same time:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.6em, itemsep=0.15em, topsep=0.25em]
	\item \textit{Loss of local equilibrium.} In attached wall-bounded flow, mean shear, turbulence production, and near-wall destruction are often approximately local. After separation, the wall layer detaches and turbulence generated in the shear layer is transported downstream before it acts on the mean flow.
	\item \textit{Separated shear-layer dynamics.} The recirculation zone is bounded by a free shear layer rather than by an attached wall layer. Reattachment depends on shear-layer growth, entrainment, and downstream pressure recovery, so small closure differences can shift the predicted reattachment point.
	\item \textit{Stress anisotropy and unsteadiness.} Linear eddy-viscosity models such as SA, standard $k-\omega$, and SST $k-\omega$ relate the Reynolds stresses to the mean strain through an effective scalar viscosity based on the Boussinesq approximation \cite{Pope2000,Versteeg2007}. This can capture additional mixing, but not the full anisotropy, coherent unsteadiness, streamline curvature of the separated shear layer, or turbulent-stress redistribution during reattachment.
	\item \textit{Model-sensitive engineering quantities.} Integral quantities such as approximate reattachment length or pressure-recovery trend may still be reasonable, while wall shear, velocity recovery, and local loss predictions become more sensitive to the chosen closure.
\end{itemize}

This distinction is important for topology optimization: a design with local separation may appear favorable if the chosen closure overpredicts reattachment or underpredicts separation losses.

The Driver and Seegmiller backward-facing-step experiment is a canonical validation case for this type of separated internal flow \cite{DriverSeegmiller1985}. The NASA Turbulence Modeling Resource comparison in Figure~\ref{fig:backstep_sa_sst_tmr} reports an experimental reattachment length of $x_r/h = 6.26 \pm 0.10$. Representative SA computations predict reattachment near $x_r/h \approx 6.1$, while the SST $k-\omega$ results reported by NASA as SSTm predict reattachment near $x_r/h \approx 6.5$ \cite{NasaBackstepTMR}. This means that SA does not simply fail for the global reattachment metric; it is comparable to SST $k-\omega$ for this integral quantity. The distinction is more subtle: both closures show visible differences from the measured skin-friction and velocity recovery, and the NASA SST case is reported as quasi-steady rather than cleanly steady on the refined grid. The backward-facing step is therefore best interpreted as a separated-flow validity check rather than proof that either SA or SST $k-\omega$ is generally reliable for all separated internal flows.

\section{Benchmark Studies in Ansys Fluent}
\label{sec:ansys_benchmarks}
To investigate where the SA model's accuracy deteriorates for the present study, benchmark simulations were performed using \textbf{Ansys Fluent}. Three internal-flow benchmarks were considered. The first two are fully three-dimensional curved-flow cases: a $180^\circ$ U-bend and a tighter $90^\circ$ pipe bend. To ensure a standardized numerical baseline for these curved cases, the computational meshes, fluid properties, and boundary conditions were adopted from the official Ansys Fluid Dynamics Verification Manual \cite{AnsysVerification2024}. Specifically, the $90^\circ$ bend corresponds to verification case VMFL030, while the 3D U-bend corresponds to case VMFL048. A third benchmark, the backward-facing step, was added as a separated-flow case without bend curvature. The results were compared directly against the SST $k-\omega$ model \cite{Menter1994}, an industry standard for adverse pressure gradients and flow separation.

For these Fluent benchmark runs, convergence was assessed using Fluent report monitors. Three physical monitors were tracked in addition to the solver residuals: the inlet-to-outlet static pressure drop, the domain-integrated viscous dissipation, and the relative mass imbalance. The pressure-drop monitor was defined from area-weighted static pressures,
\begin{equation}
	\Delta p = \bar{p}_{\mathrm{in}} - \bar{p}_{\mathrm{out}},
	\label{eq:ch2_fluent_pressure_drop_monitor}
\end{equation}
while the viscous-dissipation monitor used a Fluent custom field function,
\begin{equation}
	\Phi_D = \int_{\Omega} \mu_{\mathrm{eff}}\dot{\gamma}^{2} \, d\Omega ,
	\label{eq:ch2_fluent_viscous_dissipation_monitor}
\end{equation}
where the custom field function \texttt{viscous-dissipation-density} was defined as $\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}\dot{\gamma}^{2}$ using Fluent's \texttt{effective-viscosity} and \texttt{strain-rate-magnitude} variables. Here $\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}=\mu+\mu_t$ for turbulent runs and $\dot{\gamma}$ is Fluent's scalar strain-rate magnitude.
For a monitored scalar $q$, the final-window change over $N$ iterations was evaluated as
\begin{equation}
	\delta_N(q) =
	\frac{\left|q^{(k)} - q^{(k-N)}\right|}
	{\left|q^{(k)}\right|} .
	\label{eq:ch2_fluent_monitor_window_change}
\end{equation}
The relative mass imbalance was computed from the signed inlet and outlet mass-flow-rate reports,
\begin{equation}
	\epsilon_m =
	\frac{\left|\dot{m}_{\mathrm{in}} + \dot{m}_{\mathrm{out}}\right|}
	{\left|\dot{m}_{\mathrm{out}}\right|},
	\label{eq:ch2_fluent_mass_imbalance}
\end{equation}
where $\dot{m}_{\mathrm{out}}<0$ under Fluent's boundary-normal sign convention for these cases. Table~\ref{tab:ch2_ansys_convergence_metrics} summarizes the convergence criteria applied to the final Fluent iterations of each benchmark.
The residual criterion refers to all Fluent-scaled residuals, including continuity, momentum, and turbulence-transport equations.

\subsection{Sudden Expansion: The Backward-Facing Step Case}
The third benchmark evaluates the backward-facing-step geometry introduced in Section~\ref{sec:separated_flow_without_curvature}. The geometry and boundary conditions are based on the backward-facing-step configuration from Marcib\'{a}l's master thesis and accompanying GitHub repository \cite{Marcibal2024,joove_keps}. They are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:backstep_geometry_bcs}. The flow enters a channel of height $H_\mathrm{in}=2.0$ m with a uniform inlet velocity of $U_\mathrm{in}=25$ m/s. At $x=0$, the lower wall drops by the step height $h=0.25$ m, giving an outlet height of $H_\mathrm{out}=2.25$ m. The benchmark Reynolds number is defined using the step height,
\begin{equation}
	Re_h = \frac{U_\mathrm{in} h}{\nu} = 3.44 \times 10^4 ,
\end{equation}
because backward-facing-step results are conventionally compared using the step height and the reattachment length $x_r/h$. For reference, using the inlet-channel height would give $Re_{H,\mathrm{in}} = 2.75 \times 10^5$, but this is not the primary scaling for the separated region.

\begin{figure}[htpb]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{BackStep-Geometry-BCs}
	\caption{Backward-facing-step benchmark geometry and boundary conditions, based on the configuration used by Marcib\'{a}l and the associated GitHub implementation \cite{Marcibal2024,joove_keps}. The first 5.0 m of the upper and lower boundaries are treated as symmetry/slip segments, while all remaining solid boundaries are no-slip walls. The streamwise dimension is compressed for readability.}
	\label{fig:backstep_geometry_bcs}
\end{figure}

The Fluent setup mirrors the wall-resolved SA assumptions used throughout this thesis. The inlet velocity is prescribed as $\mathbf{u}=(25,0)$ m/s, the outlet pressure is fixed at $p=0$ Pa, and the no-slip walls enforce $\tilde{\nu}=0$. The inlet modified turbulent viscosity is set to $\tilde{\nu}=0.18449$ m$^2$/s, corresponding to the eddy-viscosity level used in the FEniCS backstep configuration. The important comparison quantity is the reattachment length $x_r/h$, obtained from the downstream lower-wall shear stress changing sign from negative to positive. Secondary diagnostics are the size of the recirculation zone, the pressure recovery, and the near-wall skin-friction distribution.

This case is interpreted differently from the bend benchmarks. Agreement between SA and SST $k-\omega$ for the reattachment length would support the use of SA for the global behavior of moderately separated internal flows. A clear mismatch would not invalidate the FEniCS implementation by itself; it would indicate that the SA closure is not a reliable physical model for this separated-flow regime. The backward-facing step is therefore used as a model-validity check for SA in Ansys Fluent before relying on SA predictions for topology-optimized geometries that contain strong separation.

\section{Conclusion}
This chapter answers the first research question regarding the predictive capabilities and physical limitations of the Spalart-Allmaras model for internal turbulent flows relevant to topology optimization. The SA model is attractive because it is a one-equation, wall-resolved closure that avoids empirical wall functions and remains substantially easier to stabilize than two-equation alternatives. The channel-like and mildly curved evidence supports its use as a practical forward model for wall-resolved internal-flow topology optimization.

The limitation is not simply high Reynolds number. The main issue is curvature- and separation-driven Reynolds-stress anisotropy under a scalar eddy-viscosity closure. Literature cases at $De \approx 9,000$ and $De \approx 30,000$ provide useful supporting evidence, while the thesis-specific Ansys comparisons bracket the present operating range: the U-bend case at $De \approx 15,000$ remains credible, whereas the tighter $90^\circ$ bend at $De \approx 18,000$ already shows clear SA deterioration for the selected profile comparison. The backward-facing step complements these curved benchmarks by testing separated reattachment behavior through $x_r/h$ rather than Dean number. These values and diagnostics are treated as case-specific guidance, not universal thresholds.

Consequently, the SA model is suitable as the baseline turbulence model for the FEniCS topology optimization framework, but final topologies cannot be accepted on the SA result alone. Any complex manifold generated by the SA optimizer must be re-analyzed with an independent turbulence closure, such as SST $k-\omega$, to assess whether the optimized low-loss behavior survives a change in turbulence model.
