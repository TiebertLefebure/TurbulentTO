\chapter{Verification of the Spalart-Allmaras Solver for Turbulent Internal Flows}
\label{ch:sa_verification}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:sa_verification_introduction}
Chapter~\ref{ch:fenics_implementation} introduced the numerical implementation of the Spalart-Allmaras (SA) model in FEniCS. Before using this solver inside the topology optimization framework, the forward fluid solver is verified against independent Ansys Fluent calculations on two benchmark flows. The first benchmark is a fully developed turbulent channel, which isolates wall damping, turbulence production, and Picard coupling in a simple geometry. The second benchmark is a curved U-bend based on the VMFL048 operating point discussed in Chapter~\ref{ch:turbulence_modeling}, where SA remained credible in the Ansys comparison. It therefore tests the same implementation under stronger convection, streamline curvature, and possible local separation, without turning the verification case into a validation of strongly separated flow.

This chapter keeps the essential benchmark definitions in the main text: geometry type, boundary conditions, Reynolds number, wall resolution, and the comparison metrics used to judge agreement. Detailed mesh hierarchies, solver controls, and configuration tables are collected in Appendix~\ref{app:B_sa_solver_verification}. The purpose is to verify the forward-flow solver developed in Chapter~\ref{ch:fenics_implementation}, because this is the turbulent-flow core on which the topology optimization framework is built.

\section{Channel Verification Benchmark}
\label{sec:sa_channel_verification}
To verify the fundamental correctness of the custom fluid and turbulence solvers, the code was first tested on a standard 2D turbulent channel flow. To cleanly isolate turbulence production, wall damping, and Picard coupling without the artifacts of a developing boundary layer, this benchmark was formulated as a pressure-driven periodic channel rather than a conventional inlet-outlet duct.

Because the SA formulation is used here as a low-Reynolds wall-resolved model, the mesh must resolve the viscous sublayer instead of relying on empirical wall functions. The channel and U-bend verification meshes are therefore designed for approximately $y^+=1$ at the no-slip walls, and grid-independence checks are documented in Appendix~\ref{app:B_sa_solver_verification}.

The same finite-element implementation choices are used for both verification benchmarks: the momentum equation is assembled with the symmetric effective-viscosity stress form, while SUPG stabilization is restricted to the SA working-variable transport equation.

\subsection{Geometry and Boundary Conditions}
\label{subsec:sa_channel_geometry_bcs}
The velocity field ($\mathbf{u}$) and Spalart-Allmaras working variable ($\tilde{\nu}$) were constrained periodically between the streamwise boundaries, while the top and bottom boundaries were treated as standard no-slip walls ($\mathbf{u} = 0, \tilde{\nu} = 0$). Flow was driven entirely by a fixed pressure drop from the upstream plane ($p=2$ Pa) to the downstream plane ($p=0$ Pa). Based on a reference bulk velocity of $U_{\mathrm{ref}}=18.5$ m/s and using the full channel height ($H=2.0$ m) as the characteristic length scale, the solver was targeted to resolve a fully turbulent flow regime at a channel Reynolds number of $Re_H \approx 20{,}300$. The detailed physical and numerical settings are listed in Appendix~\ref{app:B_sa_solver_verification}.

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:channel_benchmark}, the qualitative velocity contours of the custom FEniCS implementation show good agreement with the Ansys Fluent baseline. Both solvers produce a fully developed, streamwise-uniform channel field with zero velocity at the walls and a broad high-speed core around the centerline. The remaining visual differences are confined mainly to the thickness of the near-wall color bands and the exact width of the central high-velocity region, which are expected to be sensitive to mesh resolution, plotting scale, and the different finite-element and finite-volume discretizations. For the quantitative comparison, velocity profiles are extracted from both solvers across the middle of the channel, i.e. at $x=0.5L$, with $y$ varied from the lower wall to the upper wall. The custom solver resolves the logarithmic region of the turbulent boundary layer and predicts the turbulent diffusion toward the channel center.

Figure~\ref{fig:channel_benchmark_nutilde} shows the corresponding modified turbulent viscosity field. The two contours have the same physical structure: $\tilde{\nu}$ is suppressed at the no-slip walls by the SA boundary condition and wall-destruction term, then rises rapidly through the buffer/log region toward an almost uniform core value. The FEniCS field is slightly smoother in the wall-normal transition, but it preserves the same symmetry and wall-to-core trend as the Ansys reference. This agreement is important because $\tilde{\nu}$, rather than velocity alone, controls the eddy-viscosity field used by the momentum equation.

Because the channel is driven by a prescribed pressure difference, $\Delta p=2.0$ Pa is identical by construction and is not an independent comparison metric. The retained global metric is therefore the bulk velocity,
\begin{equation}
	U_{\mathrm{bulk}} = \frac{1}{H}\int_0^H u_x(0.5L,y)\,\mathrm{d}y,
\end{equation}
where $u_x$ is the streamwise velocity component and $H$ is the channel height.

The two error metrics are cross-code discrepancies, not separate errors for each solver. The exported fine-mesh profiles provide velocity magnitude along the wall-normal channel line; for this fully developed channel this is equivalent to the streamwise velocity within the exported precision. After interpolating the FEniCS profile onto the Ansys line coordinates $y_i$, the pointwise difference is
\begin{equation}
	e_i = u_{x,\mathrm{FEniCS}}(y_i)-u_{x,\mathrm{Ansys}}(y_i).
\end{equation}
The maximum pointwise velocity discrepancy is normalized by the maximum Ansys profile velocity to avoid singular relative errors at the no-slip walls,
\begin{equation}
	E_{\infty} =
	\frac{\max_i |e_i|}{\max_i |u_{x,\mathrm{Ansys}}(y_i)|}\times 100\%,
	\label{eq:channel_einf}
\end{equation}
and the relative profile error is evaluated as a line-integrated norm,
\begin{equation}
	E_{L_2} =
	\left(
	\frac{\int_0^H |u_{x,\mathrm{FEniCS}}-u_{x,\mathrm{Ansys}}|^2\,\mathrm{d}y}
	{\int_0^H |u_{x,\mathrm{Ansys}}|^2\,\mathrm{d}y}
	\right)^{1/2}\times 100\%.
	\label{eq:channel_el2}
\end{equation}
These profile metrics are computed from exported solver data after interpolating the FEniCS profile onto the Ansys profile coordinates.

\section{U-Bend Verification Benchmark}
\label{sec:sa_ubend_verification}
Following the channel flow verification, the solver was tested on the highly convective U-bend operating point evaluated in Chapter~\ref{ch:turbulence_modeling}. This case is closer to the curved internal-flow topologies studied later in the thesis because the flow experiences strong streamline curvature, pressure-gradient-driven momentum redistribution, and may include local near-wall separation. It was selected from the VMFL048 comparison because SA performed reasonably there; the more severe VMFL030 bend and the backward-facing-step evidence remain the cautionary cases for stronger curvature- and separation-sensitive predictions.

\subsection{Geometry and Boundary Conditions}
\label{subsec:sa_ubend_geometry_bcs}
The verification used a 2D geometry corresponding to the symmetry mid-plane of the full 3D VMFL048 case. Since the FEniCS domain is mathematically planar, it cannot resolve true 3D Dean secondary vortices. The Dean number is therefore retained only as an inherited descriptor of the corresponding 3D curved-pipe operating point, not as evidence that the 2D model contains Dean-vortex physics. The straight inlet and outlet legs are shorter than in the original VMFL048 Ansys mesh to reduce meshing and computational effort; both the FEniCS and Ansys verification runs use this same short-leg U-bend geometry.

To drive the flow, a uniform prescribed velocity ($\mathbf{u}_{in} = 1.42$ m/s) was imposed at the inlet alongside a fully turbulent boundary value ($\tilde{\nu}_{in} = 9.34 \times 10^{-5}$ m$^2$/s), while a zero relative static pressure ($p=0$ Pa) was assigned to the outlet. While the computational domain is 2D, the 3D physical pipe diameter ($D = 0.028$ m) and radius of curvature ($R_c = 0.125$ m) were retained as the characteristic length scales. This gives the same nominal Reynolds number ($Re_D \approx 44{,}700$) and reference Dean number ($De \approx 15{,}000$) as the 3D VMFL048 operating point. The detailed setup is listed in Appendix~\ref{app:B_sa_solver_verification}.

As in the channel benchmark, the U-bend mesh must be wall resolved because the SA model is integrated to the wall rather than coupled to wall functions. The mesh hierarchy and grid-independence information are kept in Appendix~\ref{app:B_sa_solver_verification}.

The U-bend is significantly more convection dominated than the periodic channel benchmark, but it uses the same SA-transport SUPG stabilization described in Chapter~\ref{ch:fenics_implementation}. This stabilization is applied only to the transported working variable $\tilde{\nu}$; no additional SUPG term is introduced in the momentum equation.

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ubend_verification}, the velocity-magnitude contours do not show a large recirculation bubble or extensive detached region in either solver. This does not rule out a small local separation region near the bend wall, because separation is more directly identified from wall shear, streamlines, or velocity-vector reversal than from velocity magnitude alone. The plotted fields mainly show that both solvers recover the same curvature-dominated velocity structure: low-speed wall layers and a broad high-speed core convected through the bend. This is consistent with the VMFL048 evidence in Chapter~\ref{ch:turbulence_modeling}, where SA behaved reasonably for this moderate-curvature U-bend despite its known limitations in separated flows. The most relevant differences are local: the bend-exit profile shows Ansys predicting slightly higher velocity over part of the inner-side half of the diameter, while FEniCS is slightly higher over part of the outer-side half. The two curves nevertheless overlap closely over most of the diameter and reach nearly the same peak velocity.

Figure~\ref{fig:ubend_verification_nutilde} provides the companion check on the SA transport variable. The two fields share the same organization as in the channel case: $\tilde{\nu}$ is suppressed at the walls and rises in the interior where the modeled turbulent diffusion is active. The important difference is the magnitude. The FEniCS contour peaks around $1.8\times10^{-4}$ m$^2$/s, while the Ansys contour peaks around $1.1\times10^{-4}$ m$^2$/s. This should not be read as a different turbulence model equation, but as a local cross-code discrepancy in the transported working variable caused by the combined effects of mesh, finite-element versus finite-volume discretization, pressure-velocity coupling, stabilization, and boundary treatment. Since the velocity profile and pressure drop remain close, the discrepancy is acceptable for forward-solver verification, but it prevents a stronger claim of pointwise turbulent-viscosity agreement.

For the U-bend, the inlet velocity is prescribed rather than produced by a fixed pressure gradient, so pressure drop remains a meaningful global comparison metric. In FEniCS, the pressure-drop diagnostic is evaluated from mean pressures on the inlet and outlet boundaries,
\begin{equation}
	\Delta p_{\mathrm{FEniCS}}
	= \rho\left[
	\frac{1}{|\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}|}\int_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}} p_k \, \mathrm{d}\Gamma
	-
	\frac{1}{|\Gamma_{\mathrm{out}}|}\int_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{out}}} p_k \, \mathrm{d}\Gamma
	\right],
\end{equation}
	where $p_k$ is the kinematic pressure solved by the IPCS formulation, $\rho=997$ kg/m$^3$, and $|\Gamma|=\int_\Gamma 1\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma$. The density factor converts the kinematic pressure difference to pascals. The same local comparison metrics used for the channel are computed from the exported bend-exit profiles as
\begin{equation}
	E_{\infty} =
	\frac{\max_i \left||\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{FEniCS}}(s_i)|-|\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{Ansys}}(s_i)|\right|}
	{\max_i |\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{Ansys}}(s_i)|}\times 100\%,
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
	E_{L_2} =
	\left(
	\frac{\int_{\Gamma_D} \left(|\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{FEniCS}}|-|\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{Ansys}}|\right)^2\,\mathrm{d}s}
	{\int_{\Gamma_D} |\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{Ansys}}|^2\,\mathrm{d}s}
	\right)^{1/2}\times 100\%,
\end{equation}
	where $\Gamma_D$ denotes the line segment across the bend-exit diameter and $s$ is the corresponding arclength coordinate. As in the channel case, the profile norms are evaluated from exported solver data after interpolating the FEniCS profile onto the Ansys profile coordinates.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:sa_verification_conclusion}
The two verification cases answer the second research question by establishing the practical operating range of the custom FEniCS-SA solver before it is embedded in the topology optimization loop. The periodic channel confirms that the solver reproduces canonical wall-bounded turbulent behavior, including the near-wall damping and production balance that are central to the SA model. On the fine mesh, the channel bulk velocity differs from the Ansys reference by $2.67\%$, while the exported diameter profile gives $E_{\infty}=2.66\%$ and $E_{L_2}=2.64\%$. The U-bend then checks the same implementation in a curved, convection-dominated internal flow. Its pressure drop differs from Ansys by $2.79\%$, and the bend-exit profile gives $E_{\infty}=8.50\%$ and $E_{L_2}=2.75\%$.

These results should be interpreted as a targeted verification of the forward solver, not as a claim that the custom implementation perfectly aligns with Ansys Fluent or that SA is generally reliable in separated flow. The known limitations of a 2D SA model remain: secondary Dean vortices, anisotropic Reynolds stresses, and full three-dimensional separation physics are outside the present solver scope. The turbulent-viscosity fields also do not show a universal FEniCS over- or under-prediction: the channel fields are close in magnitude, while the U-bend FEniCS field has a higher local peak than Ansys. Within this scope, however, the implemented SA equations, wall-distance calculation, and segregated coupling strategy produce stable and physically plausible turbulent internal-flow fields that are sufficiently accurate for use as the forward-flow solver in the topology optimization studies.
