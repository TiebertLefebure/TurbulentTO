\chapter{Turbulent Topology Optimization Methodology}
\label{ch:topology_optimization_methodology}

\section{Introduction}
Building upon the Spalart-Allmaras (SA) fluid dynamics solver \cite{Spalart1992, Spalart1994} developed in Chapter~\ref{ch:fenics_implementation} and verified in Chapter~\ref{ch:sa_verification}, this chapter introduces the density-based topology optimization (TO) formulation used for the turbulent benchmark cases. The forward model remains the same wall-resolved RANS-SA framework, but it is embedded in a fixed-grid porous-medium design loop with Brinkman momentum penalization, topology-aware SA and wall-distance penalties, density filtering, projection, and gradient-based updates.

The purpose of the chapter is to define the mathematical ingredients that are needed to interpret Chapter~\ref{ch:benchmark_results}. More detailed implementation support, including the RAMP and Heaviside curves, the design-bound sketch, the DG0 filter form, and inlet-turbulence conversion formulas, is collected in Appendix~\ref{app:topology_methodology_support}.

\section{Brinkman Penalization for Density-Based Flow TO}
\label{sec:brinkman_density_to}
In density-based topology optimization, the computational domain $\Omega$ remains fixed while a density-like topology field identifies fluid and solid regions. In the convention used here, the physical topology value $\bar{\gamma}=1$ represents fluid, while $\bar{\gamma}=0$ represents solid material. The solid-fluid boundary is not fitted by the mesh during the optimization. Instead, low-density regions are treated through a porous-medium relaxation that strongly damps the local velocity and converges toward a sharp solid boundary after projection and extraction.

Following standard density-based flow topology optimization formulations \cite{Borrvall2003,Gersborg2005}, the momentum resistance is introduced through an inverse permeability, or Brinkman penalty, $\alpha(\bar{\gamma})$. The implementation uses the RAMP interpolation \cite{Stolpe2001}
\begin{equation}
	\alpha(\bar{\gamma}) = \alpha_{solid} + (\alpha_{fluid} - \alpha_{solid}) \frac{\bar{\gamma} (1 + q)}{\bar{\gamma} + q},
	\label{eq:brinkman_interpolation}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_{solid}$ is the solid resistance, $\alpha_{fluid}$ is the fluid resistance, and $q$ controls the interpolation curve. Other interpolation choices, including SIMP-type laws, are also used in the fluid-TO literature; Eq.~\ref{eq:brinkman_interpolation} is the convention used in the present code and results.

\subsection{Density Chain and Domain Bounds}
The optimizer updates a raw DG0 design field $\gamma$, but the governing equations are assembled with the bounded physical field $\bar{\gamma}$. The density chain is
\begin{equation}
	\gamma
	\longrightarrow
	\tilde{\gamma}
	\longrightarrow
	\bar{\gamma},
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{\gamma}$ is the filtered density and $\bar{\gamma}$ is the filtered, projected, and bounded density. In the implementation this final mapping is
\begin{equation}
	\bar{\gamma} =
	\gamma_{\mathrm{lower}}
	+
	(\gamma_{\mathrm{upper}}-\gamma_{\mathrm{lower}})
	\Pi_{\beta}(\tilde{\gamma}).
	\label{eq:bounded_projected_density}
\end{equation}
The fields $\gamma_{\mathrm{lower}}$ and $\gamma_{\mathrm{upper}}$ are not additional optimizer variables. They are cellwise lower and upper bounds used to impose prescribed non-design regions: freely optimized cells use bounds $0$ and $1$, fixed-fluid cells use bounds $1$ and $1$, and fixed-solid cells use bounds $0$ and $0$. This keeps inlet ducts, outlet ducts, separator bars, and other case-specific regions fixed during the optimization. The same masking convention is used for objective and volume integrations; the relevant regions are shown with each benchmark setup in Chapter~\ref{ch:benchmark_results}.

\section{Penalization of the Governing Equations}
\label{sec:penalization_governing}
Topology enters the governing equations through penalty terms. The Brinkman momentum term and the default SA working-variable damping use the bounded projected density $\bar{\gamma}$. The reciprocal wall-distance penalty also follows the current design density in the benchmark configurations; implementation variants are summarized in Appendix~\ref{app:topology_methodology_support}.

\subsection{Modified Reynolds-Averaged Navier-Stokes Equations}
The steady incompressible RANS momentum equation is modified by adding the Brinkman sink term:
\begin{equation}
	\rho (\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla) \mathbf{u}
	= -\nabla p
	+ \nabla \cdot
	\left[
	(\mu+\mu_t)
	\left(\nabla\mathbf{u}+(\nabla\mathbf{u})^T\right)
	\right]
	-\alpha(\bar{\gamma})\mathbf{u}.
	\label{eq:penalized_momentum}
\end{equation}
As $\bar{\gamma}\to0$, the Brinkman resistance approaches $\alpha_{solid}$ and drives the local velocity toward zero.

The final velocity-pressure state used for the frozen adjoint is represented by a steady weak residual. With $\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}=\mu+\mu_t^{fr}$ and test functions $(\mathbf{v},q)$, the residual is
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		R_{\mathrm{NS}}((\mathbf{u},p),(\mathbf{v},q)) =
		&\int_\Omega \rho(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}\,dx
		+\frac{1}{2}\int_\Omega \mu_{\mathrm{eff}}
		\left(\nabla\mathbf{u}+\nabla\mathbf{u}^{T}\right):
		\left(\nabla\mathbf{v}+\nabla\mathbf{v}^{T}\right)\,dx \\
		&+\int_\Omega \nabla p\cdot\mathbf{v}\,dx
		+\int_\Omega q\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}\,dx
		+\int_\Omega \alpha(\bar{\gamma})\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}\,dx
		=0 .
	\end{aligned}
	\label{eq:penalized_momentum_weak}
\end{equation}
When the final forward solve is performed with SNES, SNES solves Eq.~\ref{eq:penalized_momentum_weak} for the accepted final state. The adjoint is then assembled by linearizing the same residual at that state.

\subsection{Modified Spalart-Allmaras Transport Equation}
Topology-created solid regions should not retain turbulent working-variable content. A sink term is therefore added to the SA transport equation \cite{Yoon2016,Dilgen2018}:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
	\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla \tilde{\nu} ={}& c_{b1} \tilde{S} \tilde{\nu}
	- c_{w1} f_w \left( \frac{\tilde{\nu}}{y} \right)^2
	+ \frac{1}{\sigma} \left[
	\nabla \cdot \left( (\nu+\tilde{\nu}) \nabla \tilde{\nu} \right)
	+ c_{b2}(\nabla\tilde{\nu})^2
	\right] \\
	&-\alpha_{\tilde{\nu}}(1-\bar{\gamma})^{N_{\tilde{\nu}}}\tilde{\nu}.
	\end{aligned}
	\label{eq:penalized_sa}
\end{equation}
The factor $(1-\bar{\gamma})$ is the local solid indicator. The coefficient $\alpha_{\tilde{\nu}}$ sets the damping strength and the exponent $N_{\tilde{\nu}}$ controls how sharply the damping grows as material becomes solid. In the reported cases, $\alpha_{\tilde{\nu}}$ can be either fixed or advanced by a continuation schedule, while $N_{\tilde{\nu}}$ remains fixed.

In the Picard-linearized finite-element solve, the topology penalty enters as an additional reaction term. Using the previous Picard field $\tilde{\nu}^{k}$ to evaluate the nonlinear coefficients, the SA update solves
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		&\int_\Omega (\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\tilde{\nu}^{k+1})\xi\,dx
		+\frac{1}{\sigma}\int_\Omega(\nu+\tilde{\nu}^{k})
		\nabla\tilde{\nu}^{k+1}\cdot\nabla\xi\,dx \\
		&+\int_\Omega c_{w1}f_w^{k}\frac{\tilde{\nu}^{k}}{y^2}
		\tilde{\nu}^{k+1}\xi\,dx
		+\int_\Omega \alpha_{\tilde{\nu}}(1-\bar{\gamma})^{N_{\tilde{\nu}}}
		\tilde{\nu}^{k+1}\xi\,dx \\
		&=
		\int_\Omega
		\left[
			c_{b1}\tilde{S}^{k}\tilde{\nu}^{k}
			+\frac{c_{b2}}{\sigma}
			\nabla\tilde{\nu}^{k}\cdot\nabla\tilde{\nu}^{k}
		\right]\xi\,dx .
	\end{aligned}
	\label{eq:penalized_sa_weak}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Reciprocal Penalized Wall-Distance Equation}
The SA model requires a wall distance $y$. In topology optimization, the relevant wall is not only the fixed mesh boundary; it also includes topology-created solid regions. The reciprocal relaxed Eikonal formulation therefore penalizes the reciprocal field $G$ toward a large value inside solid material \cite{Yoon2016}:
\begin{equation}
	\nabla G \cdot \nabla G
	+ \sigma_w G(\nabla\cdot\nabla G)
	- (1+2\sigma_w)G^4
	- \alpha_G(1-\bar{\gamma})^{N_G}(G-G_0)
	=0 .
	\label{eq:penalized_eikonal}
\end{equation}
The recovered wall distance is
\begin{equation}
	y = \frac{1}{G} - \frac{1}{G_0}.
	\label{eq:reciprocal_wall_distance_recovery}
\end{equation}
Thus, forcing $G$ toward $G_0$ collapses the recovered distance toward zero in topology-created solid. As with the SA working-variable penalty, $\alpha_G$ can be continued by schedule, while $N_G$ is kept fixed in the reported cases.

The weak residual used in the nonlinear wall-distance solve is
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		R_G(G,z)=
		&(1-\sigma_w)\int_\Omega |\nabla G|^2 z\,dx
		-\sigma_w\int_\Omega G\nabla G\cdot\nabla z\,dx
		-(1+2\sigma_w)\int_\Omega G^4z\,dx \\
		&-\int_\Omega \alpha_G(1-\bar{\gamma})^{N_G}(G-G_0)z\,dx
		=0 .
	\end{aligned}
	\label{eq:penalized_eikonal_weak}
\end{equation}

\section{Optimization Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:optimization_problem}
The optimization problem combines the penalized state equations with a performance objective and a volume constraint. The two objectives used in the framework are defined below.

\subsection{Objective Formulation}
\label{subsec:objective_formulation}
The dissipation objective is a volume power-loss functional consisting of physical viscous dissipation and Brinkman drag over the prescribed objective mask $\Omega_A$ \cite{Yoon2016,Dilgen2018}:
\begin{equation}
	J_D(\mathbf{u},\bar{\gamma}) =
	\int_{\Omega_A}
	\left[
	\frac{1}{2}\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}
	\left(\nabla\mathbf{u}+\nabla\mathbf{u}^{T}\right):
	\left(\nabla\mathbf{u}+\nabla\mathbf{u}^{T}\right)
	+
	\alpha(\bar{\gamma})\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{u}
	\right]\,d\Omega .
	\label{eq:dissipation_objective}
\end{equation}
The framework also supports an average inlet-pressure objective,
\begin{equation}
	J_p(p) =
	\frac{1}{|\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}|}
	\int_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}} p\,d\Gamma .
	\label{eq:inlet_pressure_objective}
\end{equation}
Here $\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}$ is the physical inlet boundary of the fixed non-design extension. The two objectives target the same physical idea--reducing hydraulic losses--but they expose it differently to the optimizer. The dissipation objective $J_D$ measures losses as a volume integral of viscous dissipation and Brinkman drag in the objective region. The pressure objective $J_p$ measures the mean inlet pressure directly; when the inlet flow rate and outlet reference pressure are fixed, minimizing $J_p$ is equivalent to minimizing the mean pressure drop, and the corresponding hydraulic power loss is proportional to $\Delta p\,Q$. In the final turbulent benchmark set, the pipe-bend and U-bend use $J_p$, while the diffuser uses $J_D$.

\subsection{Volume Constraint}
The volume constraint is evaluated over the prescribed volume mask, written here as $\Omega_A$:
\begin{equation}
	g(\bar{\gamma}) =
	\frac{\int_{\Omega_A} \bar{\gamma}\,d\Omega}
	{\int_{\Omega_A} 1\,d\Omega}
	- V_{\max}
	\leq 0 .
	\label{eq:volume_constraint}
\end{equation}
This constraint depends only on the density field, not on the flow state:
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\partial g}{\partial \mathbf{u}}=0,
	\qquad
	\frac{\partial g}{\partial p}=0 .
	\label{eq:volume_constraint_state_independence}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Continuous Optimization Statement}
For a selected objective $J_i$, with $i=D$ for Eq.~\ref{eq:dissipation_objective} or $i=p$ for Eq.~\ref{eq:inlet_pressure_objective}, the continuous problem is
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		& \underset{\gamma(\mathbf{x}) \in [0,1]}{\text{minimize}}
		& & J_i \\
		& \text{subject to}
		& & R_{\mathrm{NS}}=0, \\
		& & & R_{\tilde{\nu}}=0, \\
		& & & R_G=0, \\
		& & & g(\bar{\gamma}) \leq 0 .
	\end{aligned}
	\label{eq:continuous_optimization_problem}
\end{equation}
After finite-element discretization, this problem is solved over the active DG0 design variables. The Method of Moving Asymptotes (MMA) \cite{Svanberg1987} is used as the outer optimizer. MMA does not solve the flow equations itself; it receives the current objective value, constraint values, and their gradients with respect to the active raw design variables. At each design iteration it builds a local separable approximation of the constrained problem, solves this cheaper approximate subproblem subject to bounds and move limits, and returns the next raw density vector. The moving asymptotes adapt between iterations to damp overly aggressive changes while still allowing consistent descent when the gradient direction remains stable. In this work, prescribed non-design regions are excluded from the MMA variable vector and are restored through the bound mapping in Eq.~\ref{eq:bounded_projected_density}.

\section{Sensitivity Analysis and the Frozen Turbulence Assumption}
\label{sec:frozen_turbulence}
After the objective and volume constraint are defined, the remaining task is to compute gradients with respect to the design field. Direct finite differences over all design cells are infeasible because the number of design variables is the number of active DG0 cells. A central finite-difference gradient would require two perturbed forward solves per active cell, in addition to the baseline state, and each perturbation would require the wall-distance solve, SA/Picard updates, and final velocity-pressure solve. An adjoint method avoids this scaling: for one scalar objective, one adjoint solve gives the sensitivity with respect to all design variables \cite{Othmer2014,Papoutsis2016}.

Several sensitivity models are possible. The most complete option would be a coupled turbulent adjoint containing adjoint equations for the velocity, pressure, SA working variable, and wall-distance field, or a fully discrete adjoint of the complete IPCS/Picard/SNES update sequence. Such an approach would include the design dependence of $\tilde{\nu}$, $\nu_t$, and $G$, but it would also require differentiating the nonlinear SA transport equation, the reciprocal wall-distance equation, eddy-viscosity clipping and relaxation, and the solver controls used to recover difficult intermediate designs. This is substantially more complex and less robust than the laminar adjoint used in many density-based flow-TO formulations.

The implementation therefore uses a frozen-turbulence adjoint of the final steady velocity-pressure residual. The forward loop still solves the topology-dependent wall-distance and SA equations, but their infinitesimal design derivatives are omitted during gradient assembly. The final SA working variable, eddy viscosity, and reciprocal wall-distance fields from the accepted forward solve are treated as fixed coefficients:
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\partial \tilde{\nu}^{fr}}{\partial\bar{\gamma}} = 0,
	\qquad
	\frac{\partial \nu_t^{fr}}{\partial\bar{\gamma}} = 0,
	\qquad
	\frac{\partial G^{fr}}{\partial\bar{\gamma}} = 0 .
	\label{eq:frozen_turbulence_derivatives}
\end{equation}
This assumption is a practical compromise. It keeps the gradient computation close to the implemented final flow residual, captures the direct Brinkman design effect and the resulting velocity-pressure response, and avoids an unstable or incomplete turbulence-adjoint implementation. The trade-off is that terms such as $\partial\nu_t/\partial\bar{\gamma}$ and $\partial G/\partial\bar{\gamma}$ are missing. The resulting gradient is therefore the derivative of an approximate frozen-turbulence model, not the derivative of a fully coupled RANS-SA topology-optimization system. This limitation matters most where topology changes strongly alter turbulent production, wall distance, or separated-flow behavior, and it motivates the frozen finite-difference checks and independent CFD validation used later in the thesis.

Let $\mathbf{s}=(\mathbf{u},p)$ denote the final velocity-pressure state. The differentiated residual is
\begin{equation}
	R(\mathbf{s},\bar{\gamma};\nu_t^{fr},G^{fr})=0,
	\label{eq:frozen_flow_residual}
\end{equation}
which corresponds to Eq.~\ref{eq:penalized_momentum_weak}. For the selected objective $J_i$, the adjoint state $\mathbf{s}^*$ is found from
\begin{equation}
	\left(\frac{\partial R}{\partial \mathbf{s}}\right)^T
	\mathbf{s}^*
	=
	-\left(\frac{\partial J_i}{\partial \mathbf{s}}\right)^T .
	\label{eq:frozen_adjoint_equation}
\end{equation}
Equation~\ref{eq:frozen_adjoint_equation} is the adjoint equation. After finite-element assembly, $R$ is a residual vector and $\partial R/\partial\mathbf{s}$ is the Jacobian matrix of that residual with respect to the velocity-pressure degrees of freedom. The adjoint state $\mathbf{s}^*$ is therefore a vector of adjoint degrees of freedom. Products such as $(\mathbf{s}^*)^T\partial R/\partial\bar{\gamma}$ are ordinary algebraic dot products over assembled residual entries, equivalently the finite-dimensional pairing between a residual functional and adjoint test coefficients; they are not pointwise products of continuous fields.

The design derivative with respect to the projected density is then
\begin{equation}
	\frac{dJ_i}{d\bar{\gamma}}
	=
	\frac{\partial J_i}{\partial\bar{\gamma}}
	+
	(\mathbf{s}^*)^T
	\frac{\partial R}{\partial\bar{\gamma}} .
	\label{eq:frozen_design_gradient}
\end{equation}
The direct objective term depends on the selected objective. For the pressure objective, $\partial J_p/\partial\bar{\gamma}=0$ because $J_p$ contains only the boundary pressure; density dependence enters through the adjoint residual term. For the dissipation objective, $\partial J_D/\partial\bar{\gamma}$ contains the explicit Brinkman-drag contribution $\alpha'(\bar{\gamma})\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{u}$, while the frozen-turbulence approximation omits any derivative through $\mu_t^{fr}$. The resulting derivative is chained through the projection and density filter before being passed to MMA. Because Eq.~\ref{eq:frozen_design_gradient} is built under the frozen-turbulence assumption, the finite-difference checks in Appendix~\ref{app:C_topology_optimization_benchmarks} verify the implemented frozen derivative rather than a fully coupled turbulence derivative.

\section{Density Filtering, Projection, and Algorithmic Workflow}
\label{sec:algorithmic_workflow}
The raw DG0 density is filtered to impose a minimum length scale before it is used in the physical penalties. Since a DG0 field is constant inside each element, the Helmholtz-type density filter \cite{Bourdin2001,Lazarov2011} is written with an interior-facet jump term:
\begin{equation}
	\int_{\Gamma_{int}}
	r^2
	\frac{\alpha_{dg}}{h_{avg}}
	\llbracket \tilde{\gamma} \rrbracket
	\cdot
	\llbracket v \rrbracket
	\,dS
	+
	\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\gamma}v\,dx
	=
	\int_{\Omega}\gamma v\,dx .
	\label{eq:dg_helmholtz_filter}
\end{equation}
Here $v$ is the DG0 test function, $\Gamma_{int}$ denotes internal element facets, and $\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket$ is the discontinuous-Galerkin jump operator; this facet coupling follows the standard interior-penalty DG treatment of elliptic operators \cite{Arnold2002}. In the optimization loop, Eq.~\ref{eq:dg_helmholtz_filter} smooths the raw cell densities and suppresses checkerboard-scale changes.

The filtered density is then sharpened by a continuous Heaviside projection \cite{Guest2004,Wang2011},
\begin{equation}
	\Pi_\beta(\tilde{\gamma}) =
	\frac{\tanh(\beta \eta)+\tanh(\beta(\tilde{\gamma}-\eta))}
	{\tanh(\beta \eta)+\tanh(\beta(1-\eta))},
	\label{eq:tanh_projection}
\end{equation}
where $\eta$ is the projection threshold and $\beta$ controls the steepness. This step pushes filtered intermediate densities toward the solid or fluid limits before the bound mapping in Eq.~\ref{eq:bounded_projected_density} restores prescribed regions and produces the physical density $\bar{\gamma}$. The mask normalization and projection curves are shown in Appendix~\ref{app:topology_methodology_support}.

Figure~\ref{fig:turbulent_to_workflow} summarizes the optimization workflow. Each design iteration filters and projects the design density, restores prescribed bounds, updates the wall-distance and SA turbulence fields in the forward loop, solves the final frozen-viscosity velocity-pressure residual, computes the adjoint gradient for the selected objective, maps objective and volume sensitivities back to the free raw variables, and calls MMA for the next design. The ``Forward SA'' block denotes the accepted fields $(\mathbf{u},p,\tilde{\nu},G)$; these fields are recomputed for the current design before the frozen adjoint is assembled.

Continuation is used to improve robustness. The projection steepness $\beta$, RAMP parameter $q$, move limit, and selected penalty amplitudes can be advanced stage by stage. The exact schedules are benchmark-specific and are reported with the numerical settings in Appendix~\ref{app:C_topology_optimization_benchmarks}.

\section{Conclusion}
This chapter has defined the methodology used for the turbulent topology optimization results. The fixed-grid design is represented by a bounded density field, Brinkman resistance suppresses velocity in solid material, and additional topology penalties suppress the SA working variable and create topology-dependent wall distance. The optimization uses either an inlet-pressure objective or a dissipation objective, a density-volume constraint, and frozen-turbulence adjoint sensitivities for MMA updates.

The most important limitation is the frozen derivative: the turbulence fields are updated in the forward solve, but their design derivatives are neglected. The results in Chapter~\ref{ch:benchmark_results} should therefore be read together with the frozen-sensitivity checks and independent CFD validation reported in the appendices.
