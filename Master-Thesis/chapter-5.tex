\section{Introduction}
With the mathematical framework for turbulent topology optimization formally defined in Chapter~\ref{ch:topology_optimization_methodology}, this chapter turns to the question that matters most for the thesis narrative: not simply whether a turbulent optimization produces a different shape than a laminar one, but whether the final SA-optimized geometry remains credible when it is re-analyzed independently. That distinction is essential. A laminar-versus-turbulent design comparison is still useful as supporting context, but it is not, by itself, a convincing validation argument.

The chapter is therefore organized around a three-stage assessment pipeline. First, the turbulent topology optimization is run in FEniCS using the SA model, and the resulting design is examined through the objective history, projected topology, velocity field, and eddy viscosity field. Second, the final geometry is exported to Ansys Fluent and re-analyzed with the same SA model to quantify cross-code consistency on an identical physical layout. Third, the same geometry is evaluated in Ansys with alternative RANS closures, with SST $k$-$\omega$ treated as the main higher-fidelity comparator and standard $k$-$\epsilon$ retained as a secondary reference when time permits.

This structure is deliberately more conservative than the earlier draft of the chapter. Strictly speaking, the FEniCS-versus-Ansys exercise is closer to cross-code verification and independent CFD corroboration than to full validation against experiments. However, because the thesis supervisor explicitly framed this step as ``CFD validation'', that terminology is retained here in a practical sense, while the limitations of the claim are stated explicitly in Section~\ref{sec:validation_strategy}.

\section{Revised Validation Strategy and Common Metrics}
\label{sec:validation_strategy}

\subsection{What is Meant by ``CFD Validation'' in This Thesis}
The phrase ``CFD validation'' can easily be overstated. In the strict hierarchy of numerical credibility, verification concerns whether the equations are solved correctly, while validation concerns whether the model reproduces reality. The workflow adopted in this thesis sits between those two ideas. Chapter~\ref{ch:fenics_implementation} already established solver verification against benchmark CFD results. The present chapter extends that evidence by checking whether the final optimized designs remain competitive when the same geometries are re-analyzed in an independent commercial solver and under alternative turbulence models.

Consequently, the main claim of this chapter should remain narrow and defensible. A close match between FEniCS-SA and Ansys-SA supports the correctness and practical usefulness of the implementation. Agreement between Ansys-SA and Ansys SST $k$-$\omega$ then indicates whether the optimized design remains robust when the turbulence closure is changed. Neither step replaces experimental validation, but together they provide a substantially stronger argument than a laminar-versus-turbulent visual comparison alone.

\subsection{Boundary Conditions and Comparison Metrics}
The boundary conditions used for the final turbulent campaign should follow the physics of turbulent internal flow rather than the conventions of laminar topology optimization. In particular, uniform inlet velocity profiles are preferred over parabolic profiles, and the inlet turbulence level should be prescribed through a turbulence intensity (and, where required by the solver, a compatible length-scale or viscosity-ratio specification). This is especially important for the curved benchmark, where the earlier laminar-style inlet prescription artificially entangles the optimization with boundary-condition gradients that are not the real subject of the study.

An equally important issue is metric consistency. The native FEniCS topology optimization objective includes Brinkman penalization and is evaluated on a diffuse design field. By contrast, Ansys evaluates a sharp geometry on a body-fitted mesh. Raw objective values from those two settings should therefore not be presented as identical without qualification. The safer approach is to track two classes of quantities: the native FEniCS objective for monitoring optimization progress, and one or more common post-processed hydraulic metrics on the extracted geometry.

\subsection{Geometry Extraction and Ansys Pipeline}
The geometry-transfer step is a genuine source of uncertainty and should be treated as such in the thesis. The final projected design field $\bar{\gamma}$ must be thresholded to a sharp contour, cleaned where necessary, and meshed again in Ansys. Any difference between FEniCS and Ansys can therefore originate from at least three sources: the turbulence model, the numerical discretization, and the geometry-extraction procedure itself.

For that reason, the final chapter should report the extraction rule explicitly, including the chosen iso-value, any smoothing or CAD cleanup, and the treatment of non-design inlet and outlet segments. For SA and SST $k$-$\omega$, wall-resolved meshes remain appropriate. For standard $k$-$\epsilon$, the chosen near-wall treatment must be stated clearly; otherwise the comparison is ambiguous. These details are not secondary housekeeping. They directly determine how much of the observed difference can reasonably be attributed to the solver rather than to the preprocessing.

\section{Benchmark Case 3: The Curved Benchmark}
\label{sec:benchmark_pipe_bend}
The curved benchmark is the decisive case for the revised third research question, because it places the optimized geometry in the regime where Chapter~\ref{ch:turbulence_modeling} already predicts meaningful SA limitations. It is therefore the most important place to connect the optimization results, the Ansys re-analysis, and the Dean-number discussion.

\subsection{Benchmark Selection, Boundary Conditions, and Dean Number}
At the time of this manuscript revision, the current code base still contains a Borrvall-style pipe-bend benchmark, represented by Figure~\ref{fig:pipebend_schematic_revised}. For the final thesis, however, the preferred turbulent benchmark is the pipe-bend or U-bend configuration reported by Bayat, Li, and Alexandersen \cite{Bayat2026}. That benchmark family is a better fit for the revised chapter because it was designed explicitly for turbulent topology optimization, includes non-design inlet and outlet regions, and uses boundary conditions that are more natural for the present validation exercise.

The practical priority is therefore as follows. The mandatory curved case should be one well-executed benchmark, preferably the Alexandersen/Bayat pipe bend if it can be completed on time. The U-bend should be treated as an extension case because it adds separation physics and is highly relevant, but it should not block completion of the core validation story. If the U-bend campaign cannot be completed within the remaining thesis window, it should be moved to future work rather than left half-developed in the main chapter.

The inlet for the final turbulent campaign should be specified using a uniform velocity together with a turbulence intensity, not a parabolic inlet profile inherited from laminar topology optimization. For the curved case, the Dean number must be reported explicitly using the definition already introduced in Chapter~\ref{ch:turbulence_modeling}, along with the exact characteristic length and curvature radius used in the calculation. Otherwise the later comparison with Chapter~\ref{ch:turbulence_modeling} is too vague to be meaningful.

\section{Evaluation of the Coupled ``Semi-Frozen'' Adjoint}
\label{sec:semifrozen_evaluation}
The semi-frozen adjoint remains worth reporting, but it should be clearly subordinated to the main chapter narrative. It is an exploratory methodological result, not the principal validation route of the thesis. The right question here is not whether it looks more sophisticated, but whether it delivers additional physical value without introducing boundary-driven artifacts that make the optimization unreliable.

The current results already suggest a mixed answer. In domains with smooth and relatively unrestricted inlet conditions, the coupled formulation can produce more nuanced contours. In routing problems with restricted inlets, however, the coupled sensitivities can become dominated by artificial shear concentrations at the inlet-wall transitions. In those cases, gray regions near the boundaries are not evidence of a more physical optimum. They are evidence that the optimizer is reacting to a mathematical artifact.

\section{Synthesis and Answer to the Revised Third Research Question}
\label{sec:benchmark_summary}
The revised third research question asks whether SA-driven topology optimization produces designs whose predicted performance remains consistent under independent re-analysis. A credible answer requires four separate checks:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item the FEniCS-SA optimization must converge to a materially improved design relative to its own starting point;
	\item the exported sharp geometry must remain close when re-analyzed in Ansys-SA;
	\item the design ranking should remain qualitatively intact under Ansys SST $k$-$\omega$;
	\item any remaining discrepancy should be explainable in light of the turbulence-model limitations already identified in Chapter~\ref{ch:turbulence_modeling}.
\end{enumerate}

Table~\ref{tab:combined_benchmark_summary_revised} is structured to make that answer explicit once the remaining simulations are complete.
