\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{ch:conclusion}

\section{Conclusion}
The primary objective of this thesis was to develop and evaluate a turbulent topology optimization framework utilizing the Spalart-Allmaras (SA) turbulence model within FEniCS, specifically investigating its physical credibility and limits in internal flow routing. 

The investigation into the theoretical limits of the SA model confirmed its utility and its vulnerabilities. While its wall-resolved nature and relative numerical stability make it highly attractive for adjoint-based optimization frameworks, its reliance on the linear Boussinesq approximation fundamentally limits its accuracy in curvature-dominated flows. The model adequately resolves attached flows and moderate expansions, but loses fidelity in regimes characterized by high Dean numbers and strong anisotropic shear.

Implementation of this framework in FEniCS proved successful. By employing a rigorously decoupled Picard iteration scheme, an IPCS-based Navier-Stokes solver, and a stabilized relaxed Eikonal equation for wall-distance computation, the highly non-linear SA model was successfully integrated as the forward state engine for topology optimization. The utilization of a strictly discontinuous (DG0) density filter and Heaviside projection successfully enforced minimum length scales and prevented unphysical mass leakage across structural boundaries.

The most critical evaluation of the framework was established through independent cross-code CFD verification. Rather than merely comparing turbulent topologies to laminar equivalents, the extracted SA-optimized designs were re-analyzed in Ansys Fluent. The cross-code comparison between FEniCS-SA and Ansys-SA validated the numerical integrity of the custom solver, with discrepancies primarily attributed to the transition from a Brinkman-penalized diffuse field to a sharp, body-fitted geometry. However, re-analysis utilizing the SST $k-\omega$ model quantified the sensitivity of the designs to the optimization turbulence model. In benchmarks with moderate streamline curvature (e.g., the diffuser and double pipe), the SA-derived topologies performed robustly under higher-fidelity evaluation. Conversely, in the highly curved pipe bend benchmark, the limitations of the SA optimization became evident, verifying that topologies optimized under one-equation assumptions may be sub-optimal when evaluated against the complex secondary flows captured by two-equation models.

\section{Limitations}
An honest evaluation of this methodology reveals several important limitations:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{The Frozen Adjoint Approximation:} The optimization relies on the frozen turbulence assumption. While justified by numerical tractability and solver stability, it is an approximation. By ignoring the derivative of the eddy viscosity with respect to the design field, the optimizer cannot mathematically anticipate future turbulence production. In flows dominated by massive separation, this can lead the optimizer to sub-optimal local minima. 
	\item \textbf{Geometry Extraction Uncertainties:} The transition from a continuous density field ($\gamma \in [0,1]$) to a Boolean CAD geometry introduces ambiguity. The chosen thresholding value dictates the final fluid volume and wall placement. Consequently, performance differences between FEniCS and Ansys are inextricably linked to the manual geometry extraction process, making it difficult to isolate pure solver discrepancies.
	\item \textbf{Lack of Experimental Validation:} While cross-code verification against commercial CFD strongly corroborates the numerical implementation, it is not equivalent to physical validation. The true performance of these turbulent manifolds requires future validation against experimental pressure-drop and flow-field measurements.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Future Work}
The framework developed in this thesis establishes a stable foundation for several avenues of future research:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Three-Dimensional Topologies:} The current 2D implementation should be extended to 3D. Turbulent secondary flows (e.g., Dean vortices) are inherently 3D phenomena; optimizing in 3D is strictly necessary to fully evaluate the geometric limits of the turbulence models.
	\item \textbf{Conjugate Heat Transfer (CHT):} With the turbulent fluid mechanics framework verified, the natural next step is to incorporate the energy equation to optimize active cooling channels and heat sinks. 
	\item \textbf{Advanced Adjoint Stabilization:} Further research is required to stabilize the monolithic ``semi-frozen'' adjoint. If the mathematical artifacts at the boundaries can be mitigated, explicitly coupling the turbulence production sensitivities could yield highly efficient geometries in separating flows.
\end{itemize}
